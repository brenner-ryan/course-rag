## Lesson 1: Structural Loads in Layered Food

A sandwich is a load-bearing structure and most failures are structural rather than
culinary. The bottom slice carries the entire compressive load of every layer above it.
When the bottom slice absorbs moisture its compressive strength falls sharply, which
engineers call sogginess failure. The standard mitigation is a moisture barrier, usually a
fat layer such as butter or mayonnaise applied to the inner face of each slice.

A second common failure is lateral slip, where layers slide relative to one another under
shear. Lettuce is the usual culprit because its surface has a very low coefficient of
friction when wet. Placing a high friction layer such as cheese directly beneath the
lettuce reduces slip considerably.

## Lesson 2: Thermal Management

Heat moves through a sandwich mainly by conduction, and the layers have very different
thermal conductivities. A hot filling placed directly against bread will drive moisture
into the bread and cause sogginess failure within minutes. The recommended practice is to
allow hot fillings to cool to below sixty degrees Celsius before assembly, or to insulate
the filling from the bread with a low conductivity layer.

Cheese is a poor thermal insulator but an excellent moisture barrier, so it is usually
placed against the bread rather than against the filling. Toasting the bread increases its
stiffness and reduces its permeability, which is why toasted sandwiches tolerate wet
fillings better.

## Lesson 3: Failure Analysis and Case Studies

Post-failure analysis of a sandwich follows the same discipline as any other engineering
investigation. Identify the failure mode first, then the immediate cause, then the design
decision that permitted it.

The three dominant modes are sogginess failure, lateral slip, and catastrophic filling
ejection, where compressive load exceeds the friction holding the filling in place and the
contents are expelled sideways. Ejection is almost always a loading problem rather than a
materials problem, and the fix is to reduce fill height rather than to change ingredients.
Documenting failures in a shared log is strongly encouraged, since the same three modes
recur constantly.
