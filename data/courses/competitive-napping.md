## Lesson 1: Scoring and Judging Criteria

Competitive napping is scored across three categories. Onset speed measures the time from
the start signal to verified sleep, and is worth forty percent. Depth measures how far the
napper descends through the sleep stages within the allotted twenty minutes, and is worth
forty percent. Recovery measures how alert the napper is sixty seconds after being woken,
and is worth the remaining twenty percent.

Competitors frequently over-optimise for depth and lose more points on recovery than they
gain, because deep sleep produces significant grogginess on waking. The highest scoring
strategy is usually a fast onset to a moderate depth.

## Lesson 2: Environmental Controls

Ambient temperature is the single largest controllable factor in onset speed. Body
temperature must fall for sleep onset to occur, so a cool room accelerates onset while a
warm room delays it substantially. The competitive range is sixteen to eighteen degrees
Celsius.

Light matters less than most competitors assume provided an eye mask is used, but sound
matters a great deal, and intermittent noise is far more disruptive than steady noise of
the same volume. Many competitors use steady broadband noise to mask intermittent sounds
rather than attempting silence, which is rarely achievable in a competition hall.

## Lesson 3: Training Regimes and Common Injuries

Onset speed responds well to conditioning, and most competitors train by napping at exactly
the same time daily to establish a circadian anchor. Depth responds poorly to training and
is largely constitutional.

The most common competitive injury is not physical but circadian: aggressive training
schedules desynchronise the competitor's body clock, producing chronic insomnia that ends
careers. Coaches now generally cap training at one nap per day and mandate rest weeks. A
secondary injury is neck strain from poor positioning, which is straightforward to prevent
with proper support but accounts for a surprising share of withdrawals.
